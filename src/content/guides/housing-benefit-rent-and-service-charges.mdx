  - q: Does Housing Benefit pay for support in supported housing?
    a: No. Charges for support, counselling, emotional support and personal care are ineligible. Support is funded by a commissioner or, in non-commissioned schemes, by the provider from other income.
  - q: What is intensive housing management?
    a: It is housing management that costs more than in general needs housing because of the residents' needs. Examples include more frequent property checks, more repairs from wear and damage, more tenancy enforcement work and faster turnaround of empty rooms. It is eligible for Housing Benefit if it is genuinely housing management and the cost is evidenced.
  - q: Can Housing Benefit pay for utilities in supported housing?
    a: Charges for heating, lighting and hot water in communal areas are generally eligible. Fuel for a resident's own room is ineligible, and where it is included in the rent without a separate figure, councils apply fixed deductions. Water charges are ineligible.
  - q: Why does the council refer my rent to a rent officer?

  - q: How often should service charges be reviewed?

Rent is where supported housing meets the public purse, and where most disagreements between providers and councils end up. Getting the structure right does more for a scheme's stability than any other single piece of paperwork.

## The building blocks of a supported housing charge

A weekly charge in supported housing is usually made up of three parts:

1. Core rent: the cost of the property itself, including lease or loan costs, insurance, major repairs and a reasonable allowance for voids and bad debts.
2. Service charges: the cost of services connected to the accommodation. Some are eligible for Housing Benefit, some are not.
3. Support charges: the cost of support, care or supervision. These are never eligible.

Eligible rent for Housing Benefit is core rent plus eligible service charges.

## Which service charges are eligible

Schedule 1 to the Housing Benefit Regulations 2006 lists what is ineligible. Anything connected with providing adequate accommodation that is not on that list is normally eligible.

| Usually eligible | Usually ineligible |
| --- | --- |
| Cleaning of communal areas | Support, counselling and personal care |
| Heating, lighting and hot water in communal areas | Fuel for the resident's own room |
| Furniture and household equipment provided with the room | Water charges |
| Repairs and replacement of furniture and equipment | Meals, with fixed deductions where included |
| Communal TV aerial, door entry and laundry facilities | Personal laundry service |
| Grounds maintenance and window cleaning of communal areas | Cleaning the resident's own room, unless nobody in the household can do it |
| Intensive housing management | Transport, leisure and sports facilities |

## Intensive housing management

Supported housing costs more to manage than ordinary housing. Rooms turn over more often, repairs are more frequent, and staff spend more time on tenancy issues. Housing Benefit can meet those costs through intensive housing management, as long as the activity is housing management and not support.

| Intensive housing management | Support |
| --- | --- |
| Extra property inspections and health and safety checks | Helping a resident attend medical appointments |
| Managing more frequent repairs caused by wear or damage | Emotional support and mentoring |
| Faster reletting and more intensive void management | Help to manage mental health or addiction |
| Tenancy sustainment visits about rent, conduct and the agreement | Budgeting lessons and benefit advocacy |
| Managing communal space and resident disputes about the property | Help to get into education or work |

Some roles cover both. If a member of staff spends part of their week on housing management and part on support, keep time records and apportion the cost. A rough guess written into a spreadsheet is not evidence.

Councils compare your support funding with your intensive housing management charge. If a commissioner already pays for weekly key work sessions, the same sessions cannot also appear as housing management in the rent.

## How councils decide whether the rent is reasonable

For exempt accommodation, the starting point is the actual rent. The council can restrict it if the rent is unreasonably high compared with suitable alternative accommodation, or the home is larger than the resident reasonably needs.

The Court of Appeal decided in Birmingham City Council v SS and SA in 2016 that the council only needs to compare the rent with suitable alternative accommodation. Suitable means accommodation that meets the resident's needs, including the support element, not general needs housing. Councils sometimes struggle to find a comparable scheme, which is one reason clear evidence from providers carries weight.

Some residents, including older residents, people who cannot work because of illness or disability, and households with children, have extra protection. For them, the council also has to consider whether suitable cheaper accommodation is available and whether it would be reasonable to expect them to move.

## Rent officers and subsidy loss

Where the landlord is not a registered provider of social housing, the council refers the rent to a rent officer. The rent officer's figure does not cap what the council can pay, but it does cap the subsidy the council gets back from the DWP. One Birmingham analysis described claims from registered providers as generally fully recovered, while benefit paid on non-registered providers' rents above the rent officer's figure attracted little or no subsidy.

That gap, called subsidy loss, can run into millions of pounds a year in large cities. It explains why non-registered providers often face more questions than housing associations charging similar rents.

## The evidence pack councils expect

1. A rent and service charge schedule for each scheme, line by line.
2. The calculation behind each line, with the actual costs it is based on.
3. Last year's reconciliation of charges against spending.
4. Lease or loan documents supporting the core rent.
5. Staffing and time records for intensive housing management.
6. Evidence of support funding, so the council can see it is not double counted.
7. Any comparable schemes you have used to benchmark the rent.

Update the pack each year, before the rent changes. Sending it unprompted with a new claim often shortens the decision.
