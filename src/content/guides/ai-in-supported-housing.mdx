Landlords are moving quickly on AI, from repairs triage to drafting letters and analysing data. The regulator has now said how it will judge that, and the answer is simple: it looks at what happened to the tenant, not at the tool.

## What the regulator has said

In a comment piece for Inside Housing on 13 October 2026, Kate Dodsworth of the Regulator of Social Housing set out its approach [1]. The main points:

- **No separate AI rules.** The regulator will apply its existing standards and will intervene only where the outcomes those standards require are compromised.
- **No software defence.** Responsibility sits with the landlord. If an AI tool causes a failure, it is assessed as a failure against the standards.
- **A person behind every decision.** AI can analyse and advise, but decisions that affect people's homes need someone accountable.
- **Framework first.** Landlords should have proper oversight in place before significant adoption, and be open with tenants about when AI is in use.
- **Known risks.** Inaccurate data, model errors, bias, cyber security, digital exclusion and accessibility.

The regulator is also changing how it works. Its Corporate Plan 2026-2029, published on 4 November 2026, says it will seek to increase its use of automated data analysis and emerging technologies such as AI, alongside a new digital, data and technology strategy [2]. The same plan repeats its concern about the risks of lease-based specialised supported housing [2].

<Callout type="watch" title="Who this applies to">
The RSH standards apply to registered providers, which includes many small landlords running exempt accommodation. If you are not registered, the regulator's view still matters: data protection and equality law apply to you, and councils will look at the same records and governance when [supported housing licensing](/guides/supported-housing-regulatory-oversight-act/) starts.
</Callout>

## Where technology earns its place in exempt accommodation

The biggest problems in supported housing records are rarely solved by a chatbot. They sit in the gap between what support staff hear and what the property side does about it.

1. **Hazards in support notes reaching repairs.** A resident tells a support worker the heating has gone or there is water coming through a light fitting. A form or keyword check that turns that into a repair job the same day, with a reference number, closes the most common gap we see. From 30 November 2026, Awaab's Law Phase 2 adds excess cold, electrical hazards and other hazards for registered providers' tenancies, and the clock can start when a managing agent becomes aware [7]. It does not apply to homes occupied under a licence. Our [Awaab's Law Phase 2 update](/updates/awaabs-law-phase-2-supported-housing/) explains the test.
2. **Joining up systems across organisations.** Support providers, landlords and managing agents often run separate systems. Matching a repair logged by support staff to the agent's job and the landlord's completion record shows who owns each repair, and where it stalled.
3. **Checking interests before someone else does.** Matching board members, staff and managing agents against Companies House records can show links to related companies that nobody declared. The [RSH inquiry into Ash-Shahada](/updates/november-2026-rsh-statutory-inquiry-ash-shahada-birmingham/) is about conflicts of interest and third-party payments, so this is a live governance question.

In each case the tool raises a flag. A person still acts on it, and the record shows they did.

## The guardrails to set before anything goes live

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Complete a DPIA before any tool touches resident data | Paste resident names, health details or safeguarding disclosures into consumer AI tools |
| Keep a named, trained person behind every decision about a tenancy, support level or safeguarding | Let a score decide who gets help first without a person checking it |
| Check that residents who report by phone, in person or through staff are not pushed down the queue | Assume a tool is fair because the supplier says so |
| Fix the records and the managing agent agreement first | Layer automation over inconsistent spreadsheets |
| Tell residents when AI is used and what a person still decides | Hide AI use in a privacy notice nobody reads |

### Data protection

Support records hold health, substance use, offending and safeguarding information. Most of it is special category data under UK GDPR, which needs a lawful basis and an extra condition for processing [6]. The ICO expects a DPIA where processing is likely to be high risk, and new technology applied to sensitive data about vulnerable people is the clearest case [5].

Consumer AI accounts are the practical risk. If a worker pastes a resident's details into a tool your organisation has no contract with, you cannot say where that data went or how long it is kept. Use only tools covered by a data processing agreement, set out in the DPIA, with staff told plainly what can and cannot go in.

### Automated decisions

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 rewrote the UK GDPR rules on automated decision-making from 5 February 2026 [4]. The rules are looser for ordinary personal data, but a significant decision based on special category data still cannot be made by entirely automated means unless the person gives explicit consent or a legal condition applies [3]. In supported housing, decisions about support levels, evictions and safeguarding will usually be both significant and based on that data. Keep a person in the decision and record that they made it.

### Fairness and access

Many residents in exempt accommodation have no smartphone, limited data, low literacy or a disability that makes online forms hard. A system that ranks repairs or contact by digital channel can disadvantage them. That can be indirect discrimination under the Equality Act 2010 [8], and for registered providers it touches the fairness and complaints parts of the consumer standards [1]. Test any prioritisation against the residents who report in person or through staff.

## Getting ready for licensing

Licensing is likely to test governance and records more than technology. Expect to be asked where a piece of evidence came from, who wrote it and who checked it. Before you add tools, make sure you can answer three questions for every system that holds resident data:

1. What data goes in, and who can see it?
2. Which decisions does it inform, and who makes them?
3. How would you show a council officer that a flag was acted on?

If the answers are unclear today, automation will not make them clearer.
