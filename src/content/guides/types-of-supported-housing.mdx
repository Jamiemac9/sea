faqs:
  - q: What is the difference between supported housing and supported living?
    a: Supported living is one type of supported housing. It usually means long-term homes for adults with learning disabilities, autism or mental health needs, where the person has a tenancy and receives care from a provider registered with the Care Quality Commission. Supported housing is the umbrella term for every model.
  - q: Is sheltered housing exempt accommodation?
    a: Usually not. Most sheltered housing for older people provides light-touch support or a warden service that would not meet the more than minimal support test. Extra care housing with on-site care is more likely to be assessed as specified accommodation, depending on how it is run.
  - q: What does non-commissioned supported housing mean?
    a: It means no council or other public body has contracted the provider to deliver the support. The provider funds support from its own income, usually the rent and service charges paid by Housing Benefit. Non-commissioned schemes are legal but have had much less oversight, which is what the 2023 Act is designed to change.
  - q: How long can someone stay in supported housing?
    a: It depends on the model. Emergency and short-stay schemes may be weeks or months. Transitional schemes often aim for six months to two years. Supported living and housing for older people are usually permanent homes.

People use "supported housing" to mean very different things. To a council commissioner it might mean a contracted hostel. To an investor it might mean a lease on a block of supported living flats. To a resident it is just where they live, and whether the staff turn up.

The common thread is housing plus help. The Supported Housing Review 2023, published in November 2024, estimated about 634,000 units of supported housing across England, Scotland and Wales. Of those, 215,770 were recorded as specified accommodation claims on the Housing Benefit system, around a third of the total. The rest is mostly housing for older people and schemes where rent sits inside ordinary Housing Benefit or Universal Credit rules.

## The main models at a glance

| Model | Usually for | Typical stay | Support funded by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hostel or night shelter | People sleeping rough or at immediate risk | Nights to months | Council commissioning, charity funds |
| Shared supported house | Single adults with support needs, often after homelessness, prison or recovery | Months to two years | Commissioner, or the provider's own income in non-commissioned schemes |
| Self-contained supported flats | People ready for more independence | Six months to two years | Commissioner or provider |
| Housing First | People with long histories of homelessness and multiple needs | Open-ended | Council or combined authority commissioning |
| Refuge | People fleeing domestic abuse | Weeks to months | Council duty under the Domestic Abuse Act 2021 |
| Foyer or young people's scheme | Young people aged 16 to 25 | Up to two years | Council commissioning, sometimes children's services |
| Supported lodgings | Young people living with a host household | Months to a few years | Children's services or housing |
| Supported living | Adults with learning disabilities, autism or mental health needs | Long term, often permanent | Adult social care through the Care Act 2014 |
| Sheltered and extra care housing | Older people | Permanent | Residents, care funding, Housing Benefit |

## Hostels and night shelters

Hostels are usually large buildings with shared facilities and staff on site, sometimes 24 hours a day. They take people at the point of crisis, often through council referral or outreach teams. Many residents have licences, not tenancies, because the accommodation is shared and short term.

Council-run hostels can fall into [category 4 of specified accommodation](/guides/specified-accommodation-categories/). Charity-run hostels are usually exempt accommodation or managed properties.

## Shared supported houses

This is the model behind most of the growth in non-commissioned exempt accommodation. A house in multiple occupation, often three to eight bedrooms, is leased from a private owner by a charity or community interest company. Residents get a room, shared kitchen and bathroom, and a set number of support hours a week.

Done well, shared houses give people a stable base after prison, hospital or rough sleeping. Done badly, they concentrate vulnerable people with little support in streets that already struggle. The difference is mostly about the quality of support and management, which is exactly what licensing will test.

## Self-contained supported flats and cluster schemes

Some providers group self-contained flats in one building or a few nearby buildings, with an office or staff base on site. Residents get their own front door and the support comes to them. Cluster flats often work as a step between a hostel and a general needs tenancy.

## Housing First

Housing First gives people with long histories of homelessness a settled home first, with intensive support that stays with them for as long as they need it. Support is not conditional on accepting treatment or engaging with services in a set way. Caseloads are small, often five to seven people per worker.

England ran three regional pilots from 2018 in Greater Manchester, the Liverpool City Region and the West Midlands. Many councils now commission Housing First locally. The homes themselves are usually ordinary tenancies, so most Housing First rent is paid under standard rules, not as specified accommodation.

## Refuges and other safe accommodation

Refuges provide safe, often confidential, accommodation for people who have left home because of domestic abuse, with specialist support. Since the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, upper-tier councils in England have a duty to assess the need for support in safe accommodation and commission it. See our guide to [domestic abuse safe accommodation](/guides/domestic-abuse-safe-accommodation/).

## Young people's schemes, foyers and supported lodgings

Foyers combine accommodation with education, training and work support for young people, usually 16 to 25. Supported lodgings place a young person with a trained host in a family home.

Any provider housing looked-after children or care leavers aged 16 or 17 has needed Ofsted registration since 28 October 2023. Our [care leavers guide](/guides/care-leavers-supported-accommodation/) explains what that means for providers and landlords.

## Supported living

Supported living is for adults, often with learning disabilities, autism or mental health needs, who hold their own tenancy and receive care from a separate provider. The separation between housing and care is what keeps the home outside the definition of a care home. Our [supported living guide](/guides/supported-living/) goes through the models and the risks, including lease-based landlords.

## Housing for older people

Sheltered housing offers self-contained homes with a scheme manager or alarm service. Extra care housing adds on-site care. Most sheltered housing does not meet the exempt accommodation tests, while some extra care schemes do. The 2026 government response on licensing confirmed that low-cost older people's provision for over-55s will be exempt from licensing.

## Specialist models

A few other models come up often:

- Recovery housing for people leaving drug or alcohol treatment, often with abstinence agreements.
- Mental health step-down housing for people leaving hospital.
- Accommodation for prison leavers, including the government's CAS-3 scheme. See [prison leavers and supported housing](/guides/prison-leavers-cas3-and-supported-housing/).
- Accommodation for veterans, and for people with no recourse to public funds, where Housing Benefit is not available at all.

## Why the model matters for compliance

The same house can sit under very different rules depending on who lives there and how support is delivered:

1. Housing 16 or 17 year olds in care needs Ofsted registration.
2. Providing personal care to residents brings in the Care Quality Commission.
3. Five or more unrelated people sharing facilities usually needs a mandatory HMO licence, unless the landlord is a registered provider.
4. From 2027, supported exempt accommodation is expected to need a licence under the 2023 Act.

Pick the model first, then check every regime that comes with it.
