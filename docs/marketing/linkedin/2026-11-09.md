## Monday 9 November (posted today): Awaab's Law Phase 2 and supported housing

Awaab's Law Phase 2 starts on 30 November. A lot of exempt accommodation won't be covered by it, and it's easy to assume it will be.

Two questions decide it.

Is the landlord a registered provider? Awaab's Law applies to registered providers of social housing.

Is the resident on a tenancy or a licence? The government guidance is clear: it does not apply to housing occupied under a licence.

If it's a registered provider and a tenancy, the deadlines are tight. A significant hazard has to be investigated within 10 working days and made safe within 5 working days after that. Emergencies, 24 hours. From 30 November that covers excess cold and heat, electrical hazards, falls, fire, structural collapse, and hygiene and pests.

The bit I'd flag for anyone in a lease-based scheme: the guidance says a managing agent becoming aware of a hazard is likely to count as the landlord becoming aware.

So when a resident tells their support worker the heating has gone, the clock may already be running. If that note sits in the support file and never reaches repairs, nobody is acting on it.

Worth doing this week: pull ten recent session notes and check every hazard mentioned has a repair reference next to it.

## Tuesday 10 November: session notes that pass an audit

A support file can have a note for every week of the year and still not show that the support matched what the resident needed.

Usually it comes down to one missing line: which support plan goal the session was for.

A session note that passes an audit has six things in it:

- the date, start time and end time
- who was there
- what was done, in a sentence or two
- which support plan goal it relates to, in the plan's own words
- any risk or concern raised, and what happened to it
- the next action, and who owns it

"Met with resident, all fine, discussed money" has none of them.

This is likely to matter more under licensing. The draft licensing regulations propose a needs assessment within 4 weeks of a resident moving in. The assessment says what is needed, the plan turns that into goals, and the notes are the only record that the goals were worked on. The regulations are still a draft, and the consultation closes on 10 December.

Worth doing this week: pull ten recent session notes from different workers and count how many name a plan goal.

Do your note templates have a field for the plan goal, or does it rely on the worker remembering?

## Wednesday 11 November: what the draft licensing regulations say about staff

The draft licensing regulations don't give frontline support workers a training list. A lot of people assume they will.

I read the draft Supported Housing (Licensing) (England) Regulations 2027 for what they actually say about staff. It's narrower than you might expect.

The service manager. Draft regulation 12(3) proposes a licence is only granted if the service manager has the character, capacity, appropriate experience and skills for the role.

The nominated individual. Draft regulation 15 proposes they undertake training from time to time. It's the only training requirement in the draft.

Needs assessments. Draft Schedule 3 proposes each resident is assessed within 4 weeks of moving in, by a suitable skilled person.

That's it on staff. No list of courses for support workers, and no staffing levels.

It doesn't make training optional. Fire safety training is already a legal duty for every member of staff under article 21 of the Fire Safety Order. Registered providers have had to meet the Competence and Conduct Standard since 1 October. And if a council is judging whether a service manager has the capacity for the role, how the team they run is trained and supervised is the obvious place to look.

It's still a draft. The consultation closes on 10 December.

If the final regulations stay this light on frontline training, should the national standards be where that gap gets filled?

## Thursday 12 November: property and support side by side

The draft licensing regulations put the building and the support plan in the same schedule. Worth asking whether your two files would hold up equally well.

Schedule 3 of the draft Supported Housing (Licensing) (England) Regulations 2027 sets out the proposed licence conditions. Paragraph 13: the housing must be safe, well maintained and accessible for its intended residents. Paragraphs 14 to 17: a needs assessment within 4 weeks of a resident moving in, by a suitable skilled person, and support that meets any national standards.

On the property side, most files already run on dates. A certificate, an expiry, a named contractor, the next check.

Support plans often don't. "Ongoing support with tenancy matters" has no owner and no date.

The government's response to the regulation consultation describes what the proposed person-centred support standard expects: a tailored support plan "with agreed outcomes", setting out "the level and type of support required", with progress shown and barriers identified. Those standards aren't published or in force yet, and the draft regulations are out for consultation until 10 December.

A goal that stands up has four parts: the need from the assessment, an outcome the resident agreed, the support and who gives it, and a review date.

Worth doing this week: take five support plans and check every goal has a named owner and a review date, the same way your gas and electrical certificates do.

Should the final standards set a review interval for support plans, or leave that to each provider?

## Friday 13 November: the licensing start date is still a blank

The draft supported housing licensing regulations still don't have a start date. Regulation 1(2) says they come into force on "[date 2027]", with the date left blank.

I updated the regulation tracker on ExemptAccommodation.com this week, and that blank is the most honest line on it.

What the draft does pin down is what happens once the date is set:

1. A transition period for existing providers starts the same day.
2. A council can only designate its area once it has published its supported housing strategy. The statutory guidance says the plan is for first strategies by 31 March 2027.
3. The criminal offences start 9 months after the regulations do, or 3 months after every council in England is designated, whichever comes first.

So the clock that matters is the 9 months, not the year on the title page. That's the window for existing providers to get an application in, and the draft gives up to 3 months to fix gaps a council finds.

It's still a draft. The consultation closes at 11:59pm on 10 December, and any of this could change.

Three things you could start this week:
- Add "start date plus 9 months" to your risk register as a placeholder, rather than guessing a month.
- Find out where your council's supported housing strategy has got to, because designation waits on it.
- Pick one scheme and check every resident's needs assessment is dated within 4 weeks of their move-in date.

A question for anyone on the council side: would one national start date be easier to plan for than each area going at its own pace?

## Saturday 14 November: risk records and the draft licensing rules

Myth: the draft supported housing licensing regulations require a risk assessment for every resident.

Fact: they don't. I went back through the draft Supported Housing (Licensing) (England) Regulations 2027 this week, and the word "risk" appears once, in a definition of who supported housing is for. Not as a requirement.

What the draft licence conditions do propose is a needs assessment within 4 weeks of a resident moving in (Schedule 3, paragraph 14), and that the housing and its support meet any national standards published under the 2023 Act (paragraphs 11 and 17).

That second bit is where risk comes back in. The government response to the regulation consultation describes a proposed environment standard that expects "risk management processes are in place", and lists a "risk assessment policy and procedure" as evidence. The proposed safeguarding standard lists logs of safeguarding incidents and the actions taken in response.

So risk records aren't off the hook. They're sitting in standards that haven't been published yet and aren't in force.

The same response also says licensing guidance will tell councils not to routinely ask for support plans, because they can hold special category data. So the plan won't be asked for as a matter of routine, and it has to stand up on the day someone does ask.

Three things you could start this week:
- Add "named owner" and "review by" fields to every row of your risk management plan template.
- After any incident, review that resident's risk assessment and plan within a set time, and write that time into your policy.
- Take five files, list every risk above your threshold, and tick off the ones with a current, dated plan.

A question for anyone on the council side: when licensing starts, would you expect to read the risk records on a visit, or ask for the policy first?
