## Quick brief

- **The action.** On 3 November 2026 the Regulator of Social Housing opened a rare, independent statutory inquiry into Ash-Shahada Housing Association Limited (ASHA) [1].
- **The landlord.** ASHA is a London-based Community Benefit Society and a not-for-profit provider. It provides social housing in London and supported exempt accommodation in Birmingham [1].
- **The investigation.** The inquirer will report on conflicts of interest, whether payments or other benefits advanced third parties, and whether social housing assets were used inconsistently with the rules or the law [1]. Opening the inquiry is not itself a finding.
- **Local context.** On 1 and 2 November 2026 Birmingham Live reported resident anger at city councillors who own or are linked to exempt properties [5] [6].

## RSH triggers a rare statutory inquiry

The regulator can hold an inquiry if it suspects a landlord's affairs may have been mismanaged, if the landlord has failed to meet a regulatory standard, or if there is a risk it will fail to meet one unless the regulator or the landlord acts [1]. The power is in section 206 of the Housing and Regeneration Act 2008 [2].

Jonathan Walters, chief executive of the Regulator of Social Housing, said a statutory inquiry is "a serious regulatory tool that we only use very rarely". He said the decision reflected the severity of the concerns about ASHA and "its longstanding failure to put things right". The regulator will use the independent findings to decide its next steps [1].

ASHA has been non-compliant with the governance elements of the Governance and Financial Viability Standard since a regulatory notice on 23 July 2021 [1] [3]. On 29 July 2025 the regulator made three statutory appointments to ASHA's board, Martin Tiplady, Sarah Cameron and Waqar Ahmed, so the board had the capacity to deal with those failures [4]. ASHA has fewer than 1,000 units of social housing, so it is classed as a small provider. The regulator does not issue grades to small providers. It publishes a notice where it sees serious weaknesses [1].

The inquirer is independent of the regulator. The regulator sets the questions, and it can widen them during the inquiry. Some enforcement powers are available only after an inquiry has started, and only where conditions are met. The regulator will publish the outcome when the inquiry ends [1].

## What the inquiry will examine

The scope is aimed at governance and at who benefits from the landlord's arrangements. The inquirer has to report on whether [1]:

- there are, or have been, actual, potential or perceived conflicts of interest in ASHA's executive team or board
- the board and executive have the skills, independence and effectiveness to manage ASHA's affairs
- ASHA, its officers, employees or agents entered into arrangements, made payments or conferred other benefits to advance the interests of third parties
- social housing assets were used in a way that may be inconsistent with ASHA's policies, regulatory requirements or the law
- any officer, employee or agent contributed to mismanagement or a failure to meet a standard, and if so who
- any of those matters, or anything else the inquirer finds, amounts to mismanagement or a failure to meet the standards

That third-party question is not new. The 23 July 2021 notice said the board had failed to ensure that arrangements it entered into did not inappropriately advance the interests of third parties [3]. The same notice said ASHA's expansion into supported housing in Birmingham had resulted in more than 3,000 units of that type, reported as non-social housing. The model was short-term leases with third parties, most of them profit-making, plus a managing partner for housing management [3]. Those figures are from 2021. They are not a current stock count.

The 2026 inquiry asks whether that kind of arrangement is still how the landlord is run. It does not, by itself, decide that assets were misused. ## The local breaking point

The inquiry was announced in the same week that Birmingham Live reported growing anger at public figures linked to the city's exempt sector.

On 1 November 2026 it reported that Councillor Alex Yip, who has campaigned against poorly run exempt accommodation, owns homes in North Edgbaston that are leased to exempt providers [6]. On 2 November it reported resident anger on the street where some of those homes stand [5]. The same 1 November report said other councillors also have links to the sector [6].

In his statement to Birmingham Live, Cllr Yip said: "Poorly managed Supported Exempt Accommodation can have a detrimental impact on local neighbourhoods while, more importantly, failing the very vulnerable people it is intended to support." [6]

The articles are not part of the statutory inquiry, and they do not say the regulator is investigating those councillors. They do show the scrutiny providers and landlords should expect as councils prepare for licensing in 2027. ## Frequently asked questions

**What is a statutory RSH inquiry?**

A statutory inquiry is a regulatory tool the Regulator of Social Housing uses rarely. It can open one where it suspects a landlord's affairs may have been mismanaged, where the landlord has failed to meet a standard, or where there is a risk of failure if nobody acts [1]. The power is in section 206 of the Housing and Regeneration Act 2008 [2].

**Why is ASHA being investigated?**

The independent inquirer will report on conflicts of interest, the skills and independence of the board and executive, payments or other benefits that advanced third parties, and whether social housing assets were used inconsistently with the rules or the law [1]. ASHA has been non-compliant on governance since the notice of 23 July 2021 [3]. Opening the inquiry is not a finding that those things happened.
