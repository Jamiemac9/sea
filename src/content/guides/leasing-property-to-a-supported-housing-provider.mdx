  - q: Can I lease my house to a supported housing provider?

  - q: Will my buy-to-let mortgage allow a lease to a housing association or charity?
    a: Usually not without consent. Many lenders exclude HMOs and supported accommodation, and some only accept leases to registered providers or councils. Ask your lender or a specialist broker before you agree terms, and get consent in writing.
  - q: Who pays the rent when a property is leased to a supported housing provider?
    a: The provider pays you the rent set in the lease. Residents pay the provider, usually through Housing Benefit. If rooms are empty or a council reduces the Housing Benefit it pays, the provider still owes you the lease rent, but it may struggle to pay it.
  - q: Is guaranteed rent really guaranteed?
    a: Only as far as the provider can pay. A guarantee from a provider with weak finances is worth little. Check its accounts, its reserves and how many other leases it has signed.

Supported housing providers lease homes from private owners all the time. For the owner, the appeal is a single tenant, a long lease and a rent that does not depend on finding residents. For the provider, it is a way to open schemes without buying property. The arrangement works when the provider is sound and the lease is fair. It goes wrong when either is weak.

## How a lease to a provider works

| Party | Role |
| --- | --- |
| You, the owner | Grant a lease to the provider and receive the lease rent |
| The provider | Becomes the landlord to residents, sets rents, provides or arranges support and claims Housing Benefit |
| Residents | Have an occupancy agreement or tenancy with the provider, not with you |
| The council | Decides each Housing Benefit claim and, from 2027, is expected to license the scheme |

For the scheme to count as exempt accommodation, the provider must be the landlord and must provide care, support or supervision, or have it provided on its behalf. If you choose residents, set their rents or run the scheme day to day, a council may question whether the provider is really the landlord. Our [grey areas guide](/guides/grey-areas-in-exempt-accommodation/) explains why that matters.

A lease to a company or charity is not an assured tenancy, because only individuals can hold one. The Renters' Rights Act changes apply to the provider's tenancies with residents, not to your lease.

## Check the provider first

Your rent depends on the provider's ability to pay it. Before you agree terms:

1. **Check its status.** Is it a registered provider of social housing, a charity or a community interest company? Look it up on the Regulator of Social Housing's list, the Charity Commission register or Companies House.
2. **Read its accounts.** Look at reserves, surpluses and how much of its income goes on lease payments.
3. **Check its regulatory record.** The Regulator of Social Housing has found several lease-based providers non-compliant on governance and viability.
4. **Ask about its council relationships.** Does the council know the scheme is coming, and does the provider have a track record there?
5. **Ask how it would cope with empty rooms or a Housing Benefit cut.** A clear answer is a good sign.

<Callout type="watch" title="The regulator's warning">
Since 2019 the Regulator of Social Housing has warned about long, inflation-linked leases that leave the provider carrying the risk with thin margins. If the provider fails, residents lose their support and you lose your rent.
</Callout>

## Lease terms to look at

- **Rent and reviews.** Is the rent fixed, linked to inflation or capped? An uncapped rise can push a provider into difficulty.
- **Length and breaks.** Leases range from a few years to 20 years or more. Check who can end the lease early and on what notice.
- **Repairs and compliance.** Say who does repairs, gas checks, electrical checks and the fire risk assessment, and who pays.
- **Condition at the end.** Agree how the property must be handed back, with a schedule of condition at the start.
- **If the provider fails.** Agree what happens to the lease and to residents if the provider cannot continue.

Have a solicitor who knows supported housing review the lease before you sign.

## Mortgages

Most standard buy-to-let mortgages do not allow a lease to a company for supported housing. Many lenders exclude HMOs, supported accommodation and hostels outright. Some specialist lenders accept lettings to registered providers or councils with conditions, such as a maximum lease length and a provider that is on the social housing register.

Letting without your lender's consent breaches your mortgage terms. Get consent in writing, for the actual use, before you sign the lease. A specialist broker can tell you which lenders accept your provider type and property.

## Insurance

Landlord insurance is a business contract. Under the Insurance Act 2015 you must make a fair presentation of the risk, which means telling the insurer how the property is used. That includes the lease to a provider, the type of residents, the number of occupants and whether it is an HMO. Many standard landlord policies restrict cover for HMOs or supported accommodation, so you may need a specialist policy.

Check who insures what. The lease should say whether you insure the building and the provider insures its contents and its own liability, and the cover levels each side needs.

## Licensing and safety

If the property is a licensable HMO, it needs a licence. Buildings managed or controlled by a registered provider are exempt, but charities and CICs are not, so agree who applies. The full property rules are in our [property standards guide](/guides/property-standards-and-safety/).

Licensing of supported exempt accommodation is expected to start in 2027. Licence holders will need to pass a fit and proper person test and meet the national standards. Ask the provider how it is preparing, because a scheme that cannot be licensed cannot keep its residents.

## Warning signs

- A rent well above what similar homes let for locally, with a promise that Housing Benefit will cover it.
- A provider with no track record, no accounts or no contact with the council.
- Pressure to sign quickly, or to choose residents yourself.
- A lease that makes you responsible for everything and the provider for nothing.
- Marketing that sells exempt accommodation as a high-yield investment.
