Ask anyone who has reviewed supported housing files what separates good services from poor ones, and they will usually point to the same three documents. The needs assessment, the support plan and the support record. When those are strong, most other things follow.

## The needs assessment

The needs assessment is the foundation. It tells you what the person needs, what risks there are and what they want from their time with you. Under the licensing conditions set out in the April 2026 government response, every resident needs one when they move in or within four weeks.

A useful assessment covers:

- housing history, including past tenancies and why they ended
- physical and mental health, current treatment, and drug and alcohol use
- money, debts and benefits
- risks to the person and to others
- relationships, family, children, education and work
- what the person wants, and what has helped them before

Use referral information, but do not copy it. People change, and referrals are often out of date.

## The support plan

The support plan turns the assessment into action. It should be short enough that the resident can read it and recognise it as theirs.

1. Two to four goals, in the resident's own words.
2. For each goal, the first steps, ideally achievable in the next two weeks.
3. The support to be given, by whom, how often and where.
4. Risks and how they will be managed.
5. A review date.

<Callout type="practice" title="Small first steps">
Goals like "become independent" stall. "Register with a GP by Friday" gets done, and getting it done builds trust for the harder goals.
</Callout>

## Support records

Support records show the support happened. They are the evidence councils use when they decide whether support is more than minimal, and the evidence a licensing officer will use to test the standards.

A good record includes:

| Field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Date, start and end time | Shows how much support was delivered |
| Who was present | Shows who delivered it |
| What was done | Shows it was support, not housing management or a social call |
| Link to the support plan | Shows it was planned, not random |
| Risks or concerns | Shows risk is being managed |
| Next actions | Shows continuity |

<Callout type="watch" title="Records that hurt you">
Notes that are copied from one resident to another, all written at the end of the month, or that record "no issues" every week for a year, give a council grounds to decide the support is minimal or did not happen.
</Callout>

## Reviews

Review the plan at least every three months, and sooner if something significant changes, such as a hospital admission, an arrest, a new diagnosis, a change in benefits or a serious incident. A review should look at what has been achieved as well as what has not.

## Safeguarding

Every supported housing service needs a safeguarding policy that staff know and use, with a named lead.

- For adults with care and support needs at risk of abuse or neglect, report to the council's adult safeguarding team. The council decides whether to make enquiries under section 42 of the Care Act 2014.
- For children, including residents' children, contact children's services.
- If anyone is in immediate danger, call 999.

Keep a log of concerns raised, who was told and what happened next. Review it with managers each month so patterns get spotted. Our guide to [training for supported housing staff](/guides/training-for-supported-housing-staff/) covers the safeguarding and other training each role needs.

## Outcomes

Commissioners, councils and the proposed national standards all want evidence of what a service achieves. Keep it simple and consistent:

- length of stay
- progress against support plan goals
- planned and unplanned departures
- where people moved to
- incidents and safeguarding concerns

Use a recognised outcomes tool if it helps, but the basics above matter more than the tool.

## Resident involvement

Residents should have a say in how the service runs. That can be house meetings, surveys, a residents' panel or simply a manager who asks and acts. Record what residents said and what changed as a result. A clear complaints process, explained at move-in, is part of the same picture.
